Chord deadrise

What the angle is, and the fact that makes it the natural handle on the keel: on a cone-chain bottom it is constant along each plate. Same chine, keel chain and frame Béziers as the authoring tool.

Definition. At station x the bottom's half-section runs from the keel point K on the centreline out to the chine point V. Chord deadrise β is the angle of the straight chord K→V above horizontal:

β(x) = atan( (zV − zK) / yV )   ⇔   zK(x) = zV(x) − yV(x)·tan β

It is built from the two boundary curves only, so it is blind to the frame parameters — the section between K and V can be any shape at all. Change the frame preset below and watch the section curve move while the chord and β stay exactly put.

It is constant along each plate


The chine segment and the keel line of one cone are both rulings through the same apex
P (the chine segment is a ruling — that is the whole basis of the construction; the keel is the
ruling that lies in the centreplane). So the triangle P–V(x)–K(x) at one station is a homothetic
copy, scaled about P, of the triangle at any other station. Scaling preserves angles, so:


β is constant over each cone.


Writing the two rulings as P + a·dV and P + b·dK
and cutting both at station x gives it directly, with the x dependence
cancelling:


tan β = ( dVz/dVx − dKz/dKx ) / ( dVy/dVx )


Two consequences. First, the keel of a cone chain is just the chine pulled down by y·tan β,
with β piecewise constant — that single formula reproduces the tool's keel polyline to 4·10⁻¹⁶ m.
Second, if every βj is equal then every keel vertex lands exactly under its chine
vertex: zero rake and constant chord deadrise are the same condition. The tool's default keel
(z = −0.9 at K2, no rake) is a constant 37.57° chord-deadrise bottom, end to end.






As an authoring chart


One βj per cone is N numbers for the keel's N degrees of freedom —
a complete, rake-free chart. Each βj fixes the slope of keel line
j, which with the apex fixes the line; the keel vertices, and hence the rakes, come out as
intersections. It is the dual of the keel-vertex chart, which authors the vertices and derives
β.




    
  
  • It never degenerates. On a cone it is a direction parameter (a re-unit of the keel line's slope); on the
    cylinder segment, where the direction is frozen and slope authoring is impossible, the same formula becomes a pure
    translation — yV is constant there, so
    β just sets the constant drop. It is the one handle that survives both branches, and the
    short lever arm at the stem cone as well.
    • 
  
  • Mild, even gearing. 2.4–3.6 cm of keel depth per degree across all four cones of the demo, against
    12–14 cm per degree for the keel knuckle angle and 8–14 cm per degree for raw keel slope.
    • 
  
  • It is orthogonal to the frames, which redistribute shape inside the chord without touching it. Deadrise read
    as the tangent at the keel has neither property: it depends on the frame, and on how obliquely the station plane
    cuts the cone.
    • 
  
  • What it costs. Rake stops being authored. A difference between neighbouring
    β's drives the keel vertex fore or aft, at the rate in the table, and the existing warning
    (vertex past a neighbouring chine station) becomes a bound on how fast deadrise may change from plate to plate.
    • 
  
  • Caveat at the stem. yV → 0 there, so β is a
    ratio of two vanishing quantities; it stays finite and well conditioned as a slope handle, but it says nothing about
    depth near x = 0, where the keel is pinned to the stem point anyway.
