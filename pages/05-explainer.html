A chain of cones under a polyline chine

How a hard-chine hull whose lowest chine is a polyline gets a curved, developable bottom: one cone per chine segment, creased at the chine vertices, shaped by one master frame, with the keel authored by one height and a rake per vertex. Every figure is live: drag a 3D view to orbit it, move the sliders, read the derived numbers. Demo hull is 10 m, bow at x=0, starboard is +y, centreplane is y=0. Results marked numerical were only checked on this demo hull; the rest are arguments.

1. The plain loft is flat

The tool builds a bottom by lofting a developable between the lowest starboard longitudinal (the chine) and its port mirror. With a polyline chine that loft comes out flat, and the reason is symmetry. A developable is ruled. The surface is its own mirror image, so the mirror of any ruling is also a ruling, and the plane through a line and its mirror image is invariant under the reflection: perpendicular to the centreplane, horizontal in section. Along a straight chine segment the tangent plane is constant (section 6); it contains the segment and a mirror-symmetric ruling, so it is horizontal in section. Zero deadrise under every segment.

2. A chine segment and its mirror meet at an apex

Take a chine segment A→B and its mirror. Unless the two half-breadths are equal, the two lines meet on the centreplane at

P = A + t(B − A),   t = −yA / (yB − yA).

The cone with apex P through the two segments is developable and symmetric, so it is a candidate bottom panel with real deadrise. If yA = yB the lines are parallel and the apex is at infinity: the panel is a generalized cylinder with rulings parallel to B−A. On the demo hull segment V2–V3 is that case, so the code carries a cylinder branch explicitly. When A is on the centreline (the stem head V0) the apex is A itself.

3. One cone and its master frame

The chine segment passes through the apex, so it is a ruling of the cone, not a curve that rulings cross. Consequences:

  • One frame fixes the patch. Every transverse section is a scaled copy of every other, scaled about P. A point Q of the authored half-section (the master frame) maps to station x by Q ↦ Q + t(Q − P), t = (x − Qx)/(Qx − Px); for the cylinder it slides along B−A.
  • The keel is one straight line per cone. It is the ruling through P and the master frame's keel point. Its fore-and-aft slope is not free once the keel point is chosen.
  • The master frame is the only shape control, and it is worth making expressive. Here it is a cubic Bézier from the keel point to the chine point with a tangent angle and a handle length at each end. Keel angle 0 with a positive handle gives a horizontal keel tangent; a positive keel angle gives a V with a knuckle along the keel (fold = 2 × angle in every frame). Chine angle near 90° with a positive handle gives a round bilge. A chine angle below the chord angle makes the section hollow toward the chine, which is flare: the bottom flattens outward and throws spray. A negative chine angle turns the edge back down into a reverse curve. The presets below show the range.

4. Building the chain, step by step

The whole bottom is fixed by the chine polyline, one master frame and the keel authoring. The construction is a march outward from the master frame, one cone at a time. Step through it below; the text follows the same numbering.

  1. Chine and mirror. The polyline V0…V4 on starboard and its port mirror. Everything else is derived from these and a handful of numbers.
  2. Apexes. For each segment, extend the segment and its mirror until they meet on the centreplane: P0 = V0 (the stem is on the centreline), P1 forward of the bow for V1–V2 (half-breadth increasing aft), no apex for V2–V3 (equal half-breadths: a cylinder with rulings parallel to V3−V2), P3 aft of the stern for V3–V4 (half-breadth decreasing aft).
  3. Master frame. Choose a chine vertex, here V2, and a keel vertex K2 under it: authored height, and an authored rake fore or aft of the station. The frame is drawn in the plane through V2, its mirror and K2, so it is inclined when K2 is raked. Its shape is the cubic of section 3, sampled at NP=13 points from chine to keel and mirrored to a full section of 25 points.
  4. First cone aft: V2–V3. This segment is the cylinder. Every point of the master frame slides along the direction V3−V2 until it reaches the seam at V3, giving the section at V3. The lines swept out are the rulings; the surface between is the plate.
  5. Next cone aft: V3–V4. The section at V3 now lies on the new cone too, by construction: each of its points is projected along the line through P3 to the station of V4. So the seam at V3 is exactly shared by the two cones, watertight, but the two surfaces meet at an angle there (section 6). The keel point travels along the ruling through P3, which is why the keel under this cone is straight and passes through P3.
  6. Forward: V1–V2. Same operation from the master frame toward the bow: each frame point is carried along the line from P1 through the point to the station of V1.
  7. Stem cone: V0–V1. Its apex is the stem point itself, so the section at V1 projects to a single point: the cone closes at the stem. Because of this, the keel meets the chine at the stem and the stem keel vertex is not free.
  8. Keel and seams. The keel polyline K0…K4 is the trail of the frame's keel point, one straight line per cone, each through its apex. A seam can be any curve on the previous cone: it is defined per ruling by where along the ruling the next cone takes over, x = xV + rake·(1−u) + bulge·4u(1−u) with u = 0 at the keel and 1 at the chine. Transverse seams are the default. The slider lets you rake the seam at V3.

Two details that the steps gloss over. First, the order of the march is what makes seams free: the seam at V3 is drawn on the V2–V3 cylinder, so it can have any shape and the V3–V4 cone simply passes through it; but a seam is never drawn on the cone after it, so shapes are inherited outward from the master, never inward. Second, the rulings are the same lines in both directions of the march. Going aft from the master the section is projected away from P3; going forward it is projected toward P1. Both are the homothety of section 3 with t of the appropriate sign.

5. Using the free seam: one frame per cone

Step 8 said the seam can be any curve on the earlier cone. That freedom is worth more than a rake and a bulge, because the next cone is fixed by its apex and the seam alone: the seam is the shape control of the next cone. With a transverse seam the next cone's sections are scaled copies of the master frame; with any other seam they are scaled copies of the seam's projection from the apex, a different curve. So the same freedom can be held the other way round, which is how a designer would want it: give every cone its own frame, and derive the seam between two cones as their intersection curve.

Construction. Cone j gets a frame drawn at its aft chine vertex Vj+1, from the keel vertex Kj+1 of the authored keel polyline to Vj+1, in the same inclined plane as before, with its own tangent angles and handles. Its sections are the homothetic copies of that frame. Adjacent cones share two points: the chine vertex (each cone's chine ruling ends there; the rulings themselves are different lines) and the keel vertex (both keel lines pass through it). Their intersection is a curve through those two points, and because the two surfaces cross at the fold angle rather than touching, it is a well-defined curve from the chine vertex down to the keel vertex. The figure finds it along each ruling of the aft cone by bracketing and bisection, and checks the result from the forward cone's rulings as well. When all frames have the same parameters and no rake the seams come out transverse and the chain is the one from section 4; anything else bends the seams.

What it buys: a section shape that changes along the hull, for example flared forward, round amidships, a flatter V aft, which a single master frame cannot give because all its sections are the same curve scaled. What it costs is set by the fold. The aft cone's ruling leaves the forward cone at the fold angle, so a shape difference δ between the two frames moves the seam fore-and-aft by about δ / tan(fold). numerical On the demo the measured gearing is 7:1 to 9:1 (folds 6–9°): a few centimetres of section shape cost tens of centimetres of seam excursion, e.g. opening the aft cone's keel handle from 0.32 to 0.50 changes its sections by 2.4 cm and moves seam V3 by 22 cm, and a large shape change sends the seam past the neighbouring station, outside the chine, or makes the two cones miss each other along some rulings, all of which the readout flags. The rake and bulge of page 04 are the same freedom parametrized from the seam side, which is why a large bulge changes the next cone's sections so little.

6. Seams are creases; keel vertices sit under chine vertices

The seams are creases, whatever their shape. A non-planar developable contains a straight line only as a ruling, so along a straight chine segment the tangent plane of any developable bottom is constant. Adjacent segments carry two different constant planes, and they can only agree if three consecutive chine segments are coplanar. A polyline with real corners cannot bound a G1 developable. Reshaping the seam (rake, bulge) moves the fold by a degree or two and does not remove it: numerical the fold at the chine end is set by the chine corner and the frame tangent there, the fold at the keel end by the change of keel slope, and a seam only changes what happens between.

Sub-dividing a chine segment adds nothing. Two collinear sub-segments have the same apex, so their cones are the same cone, the new seam has zero fold, and the keel line does not bend there. Keel vertices exist only under chine vertices. Tick the box below to see it.

7. Authoring the keel: heights are ill-conditioned, rakes are not

Each keel line is pinned by its apex, so it has one degree of freedom, and the keel polyline has one per cone plus nothing else. The natural first idea, author the keel height at each vertex, was tried and works badly: an interior keel vertex is then the intersection of two lines that meet at the small knuckle angle, so its fore-and-aft position swings by many centimetres for a centimetre of height, and it runs off the hull entirely as the knuckle approaches zero, which is exactly the fair keel one is aiming for.

Author the other way round: one height at the master vertex, and for every interior vertex its fore-and-aft rake off the chine station. Each keel line passes through its apex and the vertex nearer the master, and the next vertex's height follows by intersecting the station. Heights and keel drop are derived, and the derivation is benign because a keel line is nearly horizontal: a decimetre of rake changes the derived height by millimetres. The stem vertex is the chine's stem point because P0 is that point.

8. The design, with unrolled plates

Everything together. Apexes pinned on the centreplane by the chine. One master frame (shape, station), drawn in the inclined plane through the chine vertex, its mirror and the keel vertex. Keel by one height and three rakes. Seams transverse by default with an optional mid-bulge; the inner shift equals the rake. Warnings when a keel vertex is raked past a neighbouring chine station or a keel line goes vertical.

Plates unroll exactly because each is a cone or cylinder. Cone: polar coordinates about the apex, radius = distance to P, angle = accumulated angle between successive rulings at P, with the ruling direction taken from the end farther from P (the stem cone is degenerate at its apex). Cylinder: distance along the ruling and arc length across.

9. How many cones? Smoothness, creases and drag

Two questions belong here. Could a single smooth developable do the job, and if not, why not cut the chine into many short segments and chain many small cones?

A single smooth developable cannot have curved sections and rocker. Take a symmetric, crease-free developable from chine to mirrored chine and a keel point K. The ruling through K is its own mirror image, so it is either transverse (parallel to y) or lies in the centreplane, and by continuity the type is constant along the keel. Transverse everywhere is the cylinder z = zkeel(x): flat sections. In the centreplane everywhere makes the keel itself one straight ruling. Either flat sections or a straight keel; the creases are what buy the combination.

Refining the chine gives a faceted approximation of a doubly curved bottom, and that is a legitimate design. Cut a smooth chine into N segments and chain N cones. Each cone is exactly developable and buildable at any N. What does not happen is convergence to a smooth developable: numerical the sum of the keel folds over all seams stays in the 45–70° range from 4 to 64 segments, because the limit surface (homothetic curved sections, curved keel) is doubly curved by the argument above and its Gaussian curvature has to live somewhere. So the creases are permanent. They get smaller and more numerous, they never fair out of the plate, and every seam is a real knuckle line to weld and fair.

Wave drag is not a reason to keep N small. Michell's thin-ship integral is the standard test for that. It punishes jumps in the offsets (a transom step makes it diverge) but not kinks in the waterlines, because integrating by parts turns the slope transform into the offset transform. The N-cone bottom interpolates the smooth chine at the vertices, so its offsets differ from the smooth limit by order h² in the segment length h, and its kinks are a Riemann sum of the smooth hull's curvature. numerical Computing Michell's integral for the test chine (experiments/michell_refinement.py: round-bilge frame, drop 0.35, waterline 0.3 m above the datum, vertical topsides from chine to waterline, 801 stations × 90 waterlines) gives wave resistance that converges to the smooth-limit value about as h²:

The 4-segment chain differs mostly because it is a different, smaller hull (offsets up to 0.64 m inside the smooth chine between vertices, wetted half-area 3.6 vs 4.1 m²), not because of its creases. From 8 segments up the wave resistance is within a few percent of the smooth hull and slightly below it. Thin-ship theory is qualitative for a hull this beamy, but the sign and the convergence are robust. The drag cost of more segments is viscous and practical: each transverse weld is a roughness step across the girth, and each seam is fairing work. The crease angles themselves are benign: a turbulent boundary layer follows a convex turn of a few degrees without separating, and at 8 segments the folds are already about 7°.

So the choice of N is a build trade, not a hydrodynamic one. Few cones: large plates, few welds, knuckles of 8–12° that read as deliberate chines. Many cones: a bottom that looks doubly curved, with knuckles of 1–3° that a builder can partly fair, at the price of a seam per segment. The demo hull's four segments are the given polyline; the refined chain is how you would go from that polyline toward a rounder hull while keeping every plate developable.
